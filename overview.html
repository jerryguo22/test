NYC Allergenic Tree Pollen Index (ATPI)

Do New York City's street trees put some neighborhoods' children at greater allergy risk than others?

1Species Evidence

NYC's 2015 Street Tree Census counted 651,951 living street trees. Abundance alone is a poor guide to allergy risk: what matters is pollen release per unit of tree size, not just how many trees exist. Using genus-level pollen-release ratios (share of measured airborne pollen ÷ share of trunk basal area) from a peer-reviewed study built on this same NYC Parks census plus NYC airborne pollen monitoring (Katz et al. 2024, Urban Forestry & Urban Greening):

London planetree (Platanus × acerifolia) is 13% of all street trees and 34% of total trunk area — but accounts for an estimated 56% of citywide allergenic pollen potential. Pin oak and other Quercus add another 22%. Together these two genera drive the large majority of NYC's street-tree pollen burden.

2Historical Choice

Did NYC select trees with allergies — and children — in mind? Municipal planting campaigns (MillionTreesNYC, 2007 onward; Greenstreets) prioritized species diversity, canopy resilience, and asthma-linked air-quality goals. Pollen allergenicity was not a documented selection criterion in that era, and London planetree, one of the highest allergenic-potential genera by this index, remained a standard street-tree choice for decades before awareness of its pollen load grew.

3Index Construction — ATPI

ATPI estimates relative source potential — not airborne concentration, personal exposure, allergy probability, or diagnosed disease. Its value is transparency: every tract score traces back to observed street trees.

1 · Tree basal area  BA_i = π × (DBH_i × 0.0254 ÷ 2)² 2 · Individual raw potential  P_i = W_i × ln(1 + BA_i ÷ 0.01) 3 · Relative individual index  ATPI_i = min(100, 100 × P_i ÷ Q₉₉(P)) 4 · Census tract index  D_t = ΣP_i ÷ area_t;  ATPI_t = min(100, 100 × D_t ÷ Q₉₉(D))

Computed across all 2,327 NYC tracts with street trees: 73 tracts score 75 or higher.

5Child Priority

Children are not prioritized because they have "weak immunity" — allergy is an immune response, not a general deficiency. They are a useful priority population because (a) asthma and allergy can disrupt health and school participation, (b) exposures around schools and homes are actionable through planting decisions, and (c) younger children rely on adults and institutions to recognize and respond to symptoms.

6Action for Children

Where high child-population share overlaps with high ATPI, planting decisions are the most actionable lever available to the city: favoring low-release genera near schools and playgrounds, and prioritizing future canopy investment away from the highest-potential genera identified in Section 1.
